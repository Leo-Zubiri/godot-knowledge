- **MDA — Hunicke, LeBlanc, Zubek, "MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research" (AAAI Workshop Papers 2004; read, 5 pages):** presented as developed and taught in the Game Design and Tuning Workshop at GDC San Jose 2001–2004; a formal approach to bridge game design and development, game criticism and technical game research. **Rules → System → "Fun"** is the consumption chain, with design counterparts **Mechanics → Dynamics → Aesthetics**: *mechanics* = particular components at the level of data representation and algorithms; *dynamics* = run-time behaviour of mechanics acting on player inputs and each other's outputs over time; *aesthetics* = desirable emotional responses evoked in the player. The designer works from mechanics toward aesthetics and the player experiences the reverse; games are "more like artifacts than media" (their content is their behaviour). The paper's own eight-item aesthetic taxonomy ("not limited to"): sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression, submission; examples assigning several aesthetics per game (e.g. Charades, Quake, The Sims, Final Fantasy). It states there is **no Grand Unified Theory** of games or formula for "fun" and presents the taxonomy as a descriptive vocabulary. Worked examples: Monopoly (feedback loops widening the gap between winners and losers; a 2d6 probability model to estimate progress time) and an AI "Babysitter" tag game across three aesthetic targets; conclusion — MDA supports iterative design and tuning by reasoning from aesthetic goals to dynamics to mechanics. No empirical validation is presented in the paper.
- **HEP — Desurvire, Caplan, Toth, "Using Heuristics to Evaluate the Playability of Games" (CHI 2004 Late Breaking Results; read, 4 pages):** Heuristic Evaluation for Playability, built from literature on productivity and playtesting heuristics tailored to video, computer and board games; Table 1 lists heuristics in four categories — **Game Play (16), Game Story (8), Game Mechanics (7), Game Usability (12) = 43**. Study design: one early-stage game concept prototyped in Macromedia Flash (screens only, no actual game play) evaluated by an evaluator with HEP and then by **four users in two-hour, one-on-one think-aloud sessions**; issues compared. Findings: HEP found more issues than the user study (e.g. Game Usability 11 of 12 heuristics and Game Story 6 of 8 useful; about half of the issues in Game Play and Mechanics), but user-study issues were more specific to the game (terminology, characters, verbiage, pacing/boredom/challenge) and some issues were found only by observing real play. Conclusion: HEP is useful in early design (before expensive prototypes) while **user testing is the benchmark** because behaviour is unpredictable; reliability across evaluators and designer-vs-expert differences left for future work. **Evidence limits (ours):** a single game, four users, an early prototype.
- **Valve, "Valve's Approach to Playtesting: The Application of Empiricism" (Mike Ambinder, experimental psychologist; GDC 2009 slides; read pages 1–36 of the deck):** designs are *hypotheses* and playtests are *experiments*; the playtesting goal is **fun — explicitly not bug testing, not game balancing, definitely not focus testing**; philosophy "get data early, get data often", "we don't know what's best (players do)", a feedback loop between design and playtest, and playtesting continues after shipping (gameplay stats, forums, fan feedback). *Traditional methods:* **direct observation** (+ what people do not what they say, feel for interaction; − observers can bias results, salient events slant interpretation, behaviour requires interpretation; "have a design goal"), **verbal reports / think-aloud** (people describe actions as they play, unprompted and uncorrected, alongside observation; + real-time glimpse of thoughts and motivations, brings up unnoticed details, good for "why" questions; − interferes with gameplay and can be artificial or distracting, inaccurate and biased), **Q&A** (surveys, individual and group; structured; validates playtest goals; + answers specific design questions and player intent; − group biases such as anchoring, social pressure and saliency, people do not know why they do what they do, potentially biased questions). Summary slides: "nothing beats direct gameplay observation", but traditional methods have artificial sessions, many biases, distorted (interpreted) data and a lack of empiricism. *Technical methods listed:* stat collection/analysis, design experiments, surveys, physiological measurements. **Stat collection** records gameplay behaviours (deaths, level times, friendly fire…) for objective, aggregate, quantified measurements and analyses such as t-tests and regressions (+ global trends, baselines, changes over time; − averages hide extreme cases, miss context/nuance, require rigor, can show illusory patterns); examples shown are Left 4 Dead death averages per level, a TF2 death heatmap and player-percentage achievement stats. Slides after page 36 (design experiments, surveys, physiological measurements) were not read.
